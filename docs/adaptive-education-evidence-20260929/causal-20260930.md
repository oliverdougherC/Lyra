### Representative complete narrow replies (second repeat)

These unedited replies show the related-law expansion under all three request paths
and the C2 overstatement. All other complete answers, including each full solution,
are in the linked records.

**A2**

> Scalar multiplication means you can multiply a vector by a number from the field, called a scalar, and get another vector.
>
> For a vector space \(V\) over a field \(F\), scalar multiplication is a rule
>
> \[
> F \times V \to V
> \]
>
> so if \(a \in F\) and \(v \in V\), then \(av \in V\).
>
> It must behave nicely:
>
> \[
> 1v = v
> \]
>
> \[
> a(bv) = (ab)v
> \]
>
> \[
> a(u+v) = au + av
> \]
>
> \[
> (a+b)v = av + bv
> \]
>
> Intuitively, it means “scaling” a vector: stretching, shrinking, reversing, or leaving it unchanged.

**B2**

> Scalar multiplication is the second operation of a vector space: a rule that takes a scalar (an element of the underlying field, like a real number) together with a vector and returns a vector, written $a\mathbf{v}$. Think of it as stretching or shrinking a vector by a factor, possibly flipping its direction when $a<0$, but never leaving the space. For it to count as part of a vector space it has to play nicely with addition and with the field's own arithmetic: $1\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}$, $a(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w})=a\mathbf{v}+a\mathbf{w}$, $(a+b)\mathbf{v}=a\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{v}$, and $(ab)\mathbf{v}=a(b\mathbf{v})$.

**C2**

> Scalar multiplication is the second operation a vector space carries: a rule that takes a scalar $c$ from the field and a vector $v$ in $V$ and hands back a single vector $cv$ that is still in $V$. So it isn't multiplication *between* vectors and it isn't something you compute from the vectors' coordinates — it's an external action of the field on the set, and being closed under it (along with being closed under addition) is what makes $V$ worth calling a space at all. What makes it "scalar multiplication" rather than any old action is that it plays nicely with the field's own arithmetic and with vector addition: $1v = v$, $c(dv) = (cd)v$, and it distributes both over vector addition, $c(v+w) = cv + cw$, and over scalar addition, $(c+d)v = cv + dv$.
